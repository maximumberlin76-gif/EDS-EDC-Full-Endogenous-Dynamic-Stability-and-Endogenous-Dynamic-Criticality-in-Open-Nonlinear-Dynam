## Chapter 5A — Coherence and Interaction

### Definitions

ψ(x,t) — wave function.

|ψ(x,t)|² — probability density.

φ(x,t) — phase of the wave function.

A(x,t) — amplitude of the wave function.

Wave representation:

ψ(x,t) = A(x,t) · exp(iφ(x,t))

Operational interpretation:

- amplitude describes the measurable distribution of probability density;

- phase describes relational wave structure;

- phase relations determine interference, coherence, and collective wave behavior;

- interaction with the environment may disturb phase relations and reduce coherence.

Within EDS/EDC:

microscopic coherence is treated as one physical layer that may support endogenous structural coherence.

However:

phase coherence is not identical to C(t).

C(t) is the parameter of general endogenous structural coherence determining structural integrity and dynamic stability over time.

At the microscopic level, phase coherence may be one measurable component or support mechanism of C(t), but it does not replace C(t).

### Definition 5A.1 — Coherent State

A state is coherent when relative phase dispersion between relevant components remains bounded or minimized:

Δφ → minimum

Operational interpretation:

- phase relations remain stable;

- constructive interference becomes accessible;

- collective system behavior may emerge;

- information loss due to phase dispersion is reduced;

- coherent evolution remains more retainable over operational time.

Consequences:

- constructive interference;

- collective system behavior;

- reduced phase dispersion;

- increased retention of wave structure;

- improved accessibility of organized microscopic regimes.

Important distinction:

coherence at the microscopic phase level may support C(t),

but C(t) is not reducible to phase alignment alone.

### Definition 5A.2 — Decoherence

If:

Δφ ↑

then:

- interference weakens or breaks;

- phase relations become dispersed;

- the system becomes more effectively separable into independent or weakly correlated states;

- coherence is lost or reduced;

- microscopic stability of the coherent regime weakens.

Operational interpretation:

decoherence increases destabilizing pressure on retained microscopic organization.

In EDS/EDC terms:

decoherence may reduce support for C(t),

but C(t) remains the broader parameter of general endogenous structural coherence.

## Chapter 5B — Micro-Equivalent of EDS

At the microphysical level, the EDS/EDC mapping is not a replacement for quantum theory.

It is an operational interpretation layer for structural stability, coherence retention, and destabilization under interaction.

Mapping:

S(t) — instantaneous intensity of synthesis of positive structural work through formation, maintenance, or reinforcement of coherent microscopic organization.

W_S(T) — accumulated positive structural work over an operational time interval T.

P(t) — destabilizing pressure caused by environmental interaction, noise, perturbation, measurement coupling, external fields, or incoherent interaction load.

D(t) — irreversible structural losses, decoherence effects, dissipative losses, radiative losses, or loss of recoverable phase information.

Δ(t) — instantaneous structural balance:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

C(t) — parameter of general endogenous microphysical structural coherence determining the level of structural integrity and dynamic stability of the microscopic regime over time.

A simplified microphysical interpretation may use phase correlation as a measurable support indicator for C(t):

phase correlation may support C(t)

but:

phase correlation ≠ C(t)

### Condition

Formal microscopic structural existence is supported when:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t) > 0

Real dynamic stability over time requires:

C(t) > P(t)

Operational interpretation:

- positive structural balance supports formal persistence of the microscopic regime;

- endogenous structural coherence must remain stronger than destabilizing pressure;

- coherence retention fails when destabilizing pressure exceeds the coherence capacity of the system.

If:

C(t) > P(t)

then:

coherence retention remains dynamically accessible.

If:

C(t) ≤ P(t)

then:

decoherence, transition, fragmentation, or regime loss becomes accessible.

## Chapter 5E — Casimir Effect

### Definition

The Casimir effect arises from modification of allowed vacuum field modes between boundaries.

In the idealized case of two perfectly conducting parallel plates separated by distance a, the force per unit area is:

F/A = − (π² ħ c) / (240 a⁴)

Where:

- a — distance between plates;

- ħ — reduced Planck constant;

- c — speed of light.

### Interpretation

The force emerges due to:

- restricted electromagnetic field modes between the plates;

- different mode structure outside the plates;

- boundary-condition-induced modification of vacuum fluctuations.

### Key Insight

The effect is not caused by a classical mechanical “force of medium.”

It is caused by modification of allowed quantum field modes due to boundary conditions.

Within the microphysical interpretation:

boundaries alter the mode structure,

mode structure alters physical response,

and physical response depends on the allowed field configuration.

## Chapter 5F — Medium Response

### General Form

A medium response may be represented by a response function:

χ(ω, k)

Where:

- χ — response function;

- ω — frequency;

- k — wave number.

Operational interpretation:

- the response of a medium depends on frequency and scale;

- the same medium may respond differently in different regimes;

- stability, dispersion, absorption, resonance, and propagation depend on the response structure.

### Insight

A medium is not defined by a single universal constant in all regimes.

It is defined by its response across frequencies, wave numbers, scales, and local conditions.

Within EDS/EDC:

response structure affects P(t), D(t), and the ability of the system to retain coherence-supporting regimes.

## Chapter 5G — Dissipation in Space

### Statement

There is no universal drag coefficient in space.

Dissipation in space is mechanism-dependent.

### Mechanisms

Possible mechanisms include:

- rarefied gas drag;

- dynamical friction;

- radiation losses;

- plasma interactions;

- electromagnetic interaction with charged particles;

- gravitational interaction with surrounding matter;

- scattering processes;

- tidal dissipation in extended systems.

### Conclusion

Dissipation exists, but:

- it is often weak;

- it is highly mechanism-dependent;

- it depends on density, velocity, field structure, particle distribution, and interaction channel;

- it must be modeled system-relatively.

Within EDS/EDC:

D(t) must not be assigned as a universal constant.

D(t) must be defined according to the actual loss mechanisms of the system.

## Chapter 5H — General Response Principle

Any medium responds to perturbations through a response structure:

χ = χ(ω, k)

### Cases

- simple approximations → χ ≈ const;

- dispersive systems → χ depends on frequency;

- spatially structured systems → χ depends on wave number;

- complex systems → χ varies across scale, position, regime, and interaction channel.

### Result

System behavior depends on:

- scale;

- regime;

- frequency;

- wave number;

- boundary conditions;

- local gradients;

- and coupling structure.

Therefore:

stability cannot be inferred from average properties alone.

It must be evaluated through the operational response of the system.

## Chapter 5I — Inhomogeneous Medium

### Extended Form

In an inhomogeneous medium:

χ = χ(x, ω, k)

Where:

- x — spatial position;

- ω — frequency;

- k — wave number.

### Consequences

Spatial variation of response may produce:

- scattering;

- focusing;

- defocusing;

- trajectory changes;

- local resonances;

- mode conversion;

- phase distortion;

- coherence loss;

- localized coherence enhancement.

### Key Insight

Dynamics depend on gradients,

not only on average properties.

A medium with the same average response may produce different dynamics if its local response structure is different.

Within EDS/EDC:

inhomogeneity may increase P(t) and D(t) if it produces destabilizing dispersion,

or may support C(t) if it creates compatible mode structure and stable coherence retention.
