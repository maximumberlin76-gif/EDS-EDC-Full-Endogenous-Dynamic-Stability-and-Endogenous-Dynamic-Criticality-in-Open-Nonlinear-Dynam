# PART I — Introduction

---

## 1A — Problem Statement

Modern systems are not static objects.

They are open nonlinear dissipative processes existing through continuous exchange of:

- energy;

- information;

- matter;

- constraints;

- and subsystem interactions.

However, many traditional models still describe systems primarily through:

- equilibrium assumptions;

- static-state approximations;

- or isolated-state descriptions.

This creates a fundamental mismatch between:

- mathematical description;

and

- operational physical reality.

Real systems do not persist through static equilibrium alone.

They persist through dynamically retained structural continuity maintained under continuously changing conditions.

---

## 1B — Core Hypothesis

Stability is not a property of a static state.

Stability is a property of a dynamically retained operational regime.

A system remains viable only while regenerative structural continuity remains stronger than destabilizing load and dissipation over operational time.

Within EDS/EDC:

stability emerges through retained internally agreed coherent subsystem dynamics.

This recurring structural pattern appears across:

## 1G — Core Principle

A structured system remains operationally viable only while it preserves positive structural balance and retained coherent continuity over operational time.

Two distinct conditions must be separated.

### Structural Existence

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t) > 0

This condition determines whether structural organization continues to exist.

### Dynamic Stability

C(t) > P(t)

This condition determines whether retained internally agreed coherent subsystem dynamics remain stronger than destabilizing fragmentation and dissipation.

Operationally:

- structural existence alone is insufficient;

- dynamically retained coherent continuity is required for long-term stability.
