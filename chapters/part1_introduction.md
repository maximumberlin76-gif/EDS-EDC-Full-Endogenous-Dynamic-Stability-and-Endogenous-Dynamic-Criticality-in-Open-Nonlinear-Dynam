# PART I — Introduction

## 1A — Problem Statement

Modern systems are not static objects.

They are open nonlinear dissipative processes existing through continuous exchange of:

- energy;

- information;

- matter;

- constraints;

- and subsystem interactions.

However, many traditional models still describe systems primarily through:

- equilibrium assumptions;

- static-state approximations;

- isolated-state descriptions;

- or externally fixed state variables.

This creates a fundamental mismatch between:

- mathematical description;

and

- operational physical reality.

Real systems do not persist through static equilibrium alone.

They persist through dynamically retained structural organization maintained under continuously changing conditions.

Within EDS/EDC, a structured system must be understood not only by its external form, but by the internal dynamics through which it preserves formal structural existence and real dynamic stability over time.

Two conditions must therefore be separated from the beginning:

formal structural existence;

and

real dynamic stability.

Formal structural existence is described by:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

where:

S(t) — instantaneous intensity of synthesis of positive structural work;

P(t) — destabilizing pressure;

D(t) — irreversible structural losses.

Real dynamic stability over time is described by:

C(t) > P(t)

where:

C(t) — parameter of general endogenous structural coherence determining structural integrity and dynamic stability over time.

Positive structural balance may preserve external form.

But external form alone does not prove real dynamic stability.

A structure may continue to exist formally while already losing endogenous structural coherence internally.

## 1B — Core Hypothesis

Stability is not a property of a static state.

Stability is a property of a dynamically retained operational regime.

A system remains formally existent while its instantaneous structural balance remains positive:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t) > 0

But a system remains dynamically stable over time only while:

C(t) > P(t)

This distinction is central.

Positive Δ(t) supports formal structural existence.

C(t) > P(t) defines real dynamic stability over time.

Within EDS/EDC, stability emerges through general endogenous structural coherence of internal self-organization processes.

Structural regeneration is a continuous endogenous process of restoring and maintaining structural integrity and coherence over time.

Regeneration supports C(t), but is not identical to C(t).

If endogenous processes become decoherent, regenerative capacity decreases.

If dissipation exceeds the structure’s capacity for regeneration over time, C(t) decreases.

Therefore:

decoherence of endogenous processes → reduction of regenerative capacity → dissipation exceeds regeneration → decrease of C(t) → disruption of dynamic stability over time.

## 1G — Core Principle

A structured system remains operationally viable only while it preserves formal structural existence and real dynamic stability over operational time.

Two distinct conditions must be separated.

### Structural Existence

Formal structural existence is described by:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

where:

S(t) — instantaneous intensity of synthesis of positive structural work;

P(t) — destabilizing pressure;

D(t) — irreversible structural losses.

If:

Δ(t) > 0

then the structure preserves positive structural formation balance.

This condition determines whether structural organization continues to exist formally.

Accumulated positive structural work over an operational time interval is described by:

W_S(T) = ∫[t₀ → t₁] S(t) dt

W_S(T) may support structural integrity, self-organization, and endogenous structural coherence.

But W_S(T) does not replace C(t).

Positive Δ(t) and positive accumulated structural balance do not by themselves prove real dynamic stability over time.

### Dynamic Stability

Real dynamic stability over time is described by:

C(t) > P(t)

where:

C(t) — parameter of general endogenous structural coherence determining structural integrity and dynamic stability over time.

P(t) — destabilizing pressure.

This condition determines whether endogenous structural coherence remains stronger than destabilizing pressure.

Operationally:

- structural existence alone is insufficient;

- accumulated positive structural work alone is insufficient;

- synchronization alone is insufficient;

- regeneration alone is insufficient;

- real dynamic stability requires C(t) > P(t).

Structural regeneration supports C(t), but is not identical to C(t).

Synchronization may support C(t), but is not identical to C(t).

The decisive condition remains:

C(t) > P(t)
