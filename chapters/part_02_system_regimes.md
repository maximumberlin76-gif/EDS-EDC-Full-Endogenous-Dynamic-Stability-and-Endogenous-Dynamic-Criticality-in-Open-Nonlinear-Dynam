# PART II — SYSTEM REGIMES

## Chapter 2F — Separation vs Synthesis

### Definition 2F.1 — Separation Regime

A system operates in a separation regime if:

P(t) + D(t) ≥ S(t)

Where:

- S(t) — instantaneous intensity of synthesis of positive structural work;

- P(t) — destabilizing extraction pressure, operational load, fragmentation pressure, or destabilizing demand;

- D(t) — irreversible structural losses, dissipative degradation, entropy-producing structural loss, or degradation burden.

Operational interpretation:

- destabilizing pressure and irreversible losses become equal to or stronger than synthesis of positive structural work;

- formal structural balance approaches zero or becomes negative;

- subsystem trajectories begin diverging;

- regenerative support weakens;

- endogenous structural coherence becomes harder to retain over time.

The instantaneous structural balance is:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

In the separation regime:

Δ(t) ≤ 0

or approaches the critical boundary:

Δ(t) → 0

This means that formal structural existence becomes unstable or progressively harder to maintain.

However, the separation regime must also be evaluated through the EDS stability criterion:

C(t) > P(t)

because a system may still preserve positive structural balance for some time while real dynamic stability is already degraded.

### Consequences of Separation Regime

Within a separation regime:

- local efficiency may temporarily increase;

- subsystem isolation may intensify;

- short-term extraction may dominate operational logic;

- structural connections weaken;

- irreversible losses D(t) increase;

- fragmentation pressure propagates;

- accumulated positive structural work becomes harder to retain;

- regenerative capacity weakens;

- endogenous structural coherence decreases;

- internal self-organization becomes less coherent over operational time.

Operationally:

the system may preserve temporary external functionality while internally losing structural integrity.

Thus:

external persistence does not guarantee real dynamic stability.

A possible regime is:

Δ(t) > 0, but C(t) ≤ P(t)

This means:

the structure may still exist formally, but its internal dynamic stability over time is already disrupted.

### Definition 2F.2 — Synthesis Regime

A system operates in a synthesis regime if:

S(t) > P(t) + D(t)

Operational interpretation:

- synthesis of positive structural work exceeds destabilizing pressure and irreversible losses;

- formal structural balance remains positive;

- structural organization remains accessible;

- subsystem compatibility may strengthen;

- accumulated positive structural work may increase over time.

The structural balance is:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

In the synthesis regime:

Δ(t) > 0

Accumulated positive structural work is described by:

W_S(T) = ∫[t₀ → t₁] S(t) dt

Operational interpretation:

- W_S(T) may support structural integrity;

- W_S(T) may support self-organization;

- W_S(T) may support endogenous structural coherence;

- W_S(T) does not replace C(t).

### Consequences of Synthesis Regime

Within a synthesis regime:

- structural connectivity may increase;

- subsystem compatibility may improve;

- accumulated positive structural work may grow;

- regenerative support may strengthen;

- new organizational structures may emerge;

- internal self-organization may become more coherent;

- system stability may improve operationally over time.

However:

S(t) > P(t) + D(t)

does not by itself prove real dynamic stability.

Real dynamic stability still requires:

C(t) > P(t)

where:

C(t) — parameter of general endogenous structural coherence determining structural integrity and dynamic stability over time.

Therefore:

the synthesis regime supports formal structural existence,

but dynamic stability depends on whether endogenous structural coherence remains stronger than destabilizing pressure.

## Chapter 2G — Coherent / Resonant Regime

### Definition 2G.1 — Resonance

Resonance is a regime of increased compatibility of interacting subsystem dynamics, where coherent interaction becomes more accessible and destabilizing dispersion may decrease.

In a simplified phase-based representation:

Δφ → minimum

Q(t) → maximum

Where:

- Δφ — subsystem phase mismatch;

- Q(t) — quality factor representing retention capacity of coherent structural dynamics.

Operational interpretation:

- subsystem trajectories become more compatible;

- destabilizing phase conflict decreases;

- coherent energy retention improves;

- synchronization accessibility may increase;

- retained structural organization becomes easier to support.

Within EDS/EDC:

resonance is not simple oscillatory coincidence alone.

It is a regime where subsystem interaction may reduce destabilizing dissipation and support endogenous structural coherence.

Important distinction:

resonance is not identical to C(t).

Synchronization is not identical to C(t).

Phase alignment is not identical to C(t).

They may support C(t), but they do not replace C(t).

Real dynamic stability remains governed by:

C(t) > P(t)

### Relation to Coherence

A reduction in phase mismatch may support endogenous structural coherence:

Δφ ↓ may support C(t)

But this must not be written as strict identity:

C(t) ↑ ⇔ Δφ ↓

because C(t) is not simple phase synchronization.

C(t) is the parameter of general endogenous structural coherence.

Operational meaning:

- decreasing phase mismatch may reduce internal conflict;

- reduced internal conflict may support regenerative capacity;

- improved subsystem compatibility may support C(t);

- but C(t) also depends on amplitude, direction of effort, functional contribution, accumulation rate, connection quality, and structural role of subsystem dynamics.

Correct interpretation:

Δφ ↓ may support C(t), but does not define C(t).

### Definition 2G.2 — Coherent Regime

A system operates in a coherent regime if:

C(t) > P(t)

and if internal subsystem dynamics remain sufficiently compatible to preserve structural integrity over operational time.

Operational interpretation:

- endogenous structural coherence exceeds destabilizing pressure;

- subsystem interactions remain compatible enough to retain structure;

- regenerative support remains effective;

- fragmentation pressure remains bounded;

- the system remains dynamically stable over time.

In this regime:

Δ(t) may remain positive,

W_S(T) may accumulate,

and C(t) remains above destabilizing pressure.

But the decisive criterion remains:

C(t) > P(t)

### Definition 2G.3 — Resonant Support Regime

A system operates in a resonant support regime if resonance, synchronization, or phase alignment supports endogenous structural coherence without replacing it.

Operationally:

- R(t) may increase;

- Δφ may decrease;

- Q(t) may increase;

- effective phase dispersion may decrease;

- subsystem compatibility may improve;

- C(t) may become easier to retain.

But:

R(t) ≠ C(t)

Δφ ≠ C(t)

Q(t) ≠ C(t)

Synchronization ≠ coherence

The resonant support regime is valid only as a support layer.

It becomes dynamically stabilizing only if it helps maintain:

C(t) > P(t)

## Chapter 2H — Critical Regime

### Definition 2H.1 — EDC Boundary

The EDC critical boundary appears when:

C(t) ≈ P(t)

Operational interpretation:

- endogenous structural coherence becomes close to destabilizing pressure;

- the stability margin becomes small;

- small perturbations may produce disproportionate structural effects;

- parameter drift becomes significant;

- regime transition becomes accessible.

This is not yet necessarily collapse.

It is the boundary where the system becomes dynamically sensitive.

### Consequences of Critical Regime

Near:

C(t) ≈ P(t)

the system may exhibit:

- increased sensitivity to perturbation;

- delayed transition;

- nonlinear amplification;

- subsystem incompatibility growth;

- sudden degradation;

- reorganization;

- phase transition;

- or collapse into a lower-order regime.

The transition may be positive or negative.

A critical window is not automatically a synthesis window.

A resonance window is not automatically a positive transition.

The sign and quality of the transition depend on endogenous drift.

### Positive Critical Transition

A positive critical transition may occur if:

- C(t) recovers above P(t);

- accumulated positive structural work supports reorganization;

- subsystem compatibility increases;

- regenerative support strengthens;

- irreversible losses remain bounded.

Operationally:

C(t) > P(t)

is restored after transition.

The system may enter a higher retained regime.

### Negative Critical Transition

A negative critical transition may occur if:

- C(t) falls below P(t);

- irreversible losses increase;

- fragmentation propagates;

- regenerative capacity weakens;

- subsystem compatibility collapses.

Operationally:

C(t) ≤ P(t)

leads to disruption of real dynamic stability.

If additionally:

S(t) < P(t) + D(t)

then:

Δ(t) < 0

and formal structural balance also becomes negative.

## Chapter 2I — Regime Map

### Regime 1 — Formal Existence with Dynamic Stability

Condition:

Δ(t) > 0

and:

C(t) > P(t)

Operational meaning:

- formal structural existence is preserved;

- real dynamic stability is preserved;

- synthesis of positive structural work exceeds losses and pressure;

- endogenous structural coherence exceeds destabilizing pressure.

This is the strongest retained regime.

### Regime 2 — Formal Existence with Internal Degradation

Condition:

Δ(t) > 0

but:

C(t) ≤ P(t)

Operational meaning:

- the structure still exists formally;

- external function may still be observable;

- internal dynamic stability is already disrupted;

- endogenous structural coherence is insufficient;

- degradation may be hidden beneath external persistence.

This regime is diagnostically important.

### Regime 3 — Quasi-Stationary Balance

Condition:

Δ(t) ≈ 0

Operational meaning:

- synthesis approximately compensates pressure and losses;

- the system may appear stable externally;

- no strong positive structural surplus is accumulating;

- small perturbations may shift the system toward degradation or recovery.

Real dynamic stability still depends on:

C(t) > P(t)

### Regime 4 — Critical EDC Boundary

Condition:

C(t) ≈ P(t)

Operational meaning:

- dynamic stability margin becomes small;

- the system becomes sensitive to perturbation;

- transition becomes accessible;

- delayed nonlinear response may appear.

### Regime 5 — Degradation Regime

Condition:

Δ(t) < 0

and/or:

C(t) < P(t)

Operational meaning:

- formal structural balance becomes negative;

- real dynamic stability is disrupted;

- irreversible losses and destabilizing pressure dominate;

- fragmentation and collapse become accessible.

### Regime 6 — Reorganization Regime

Condition:

C(t) recovers above P(t)

after critical transition.

Operational meaning:

- endogenous structural coherence is restored;

- accumulated positive structural work may be reorganized into a new retained structure;

- the system may enter a new dynamically stable regime.

## Chapter 2J — Final Principle

Separation, synthesis, resonance, criticality, degradation, and reorganization must be evaluated through two distinct layers:

### Layer 1 — Formal Structural Existence

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

This determines whether structural balance remains positive.

### Layer 2 — Real Dynamic Stability

C(t) > P(t)

This determines whether endogenous structural coherence remains stronger than destabilizing pressure.

Operationally:

- synthesis supports formal structural existence;

- accumulated positive structural work may support endogenous structural coherence;

- resonance may support synchronization and coherence;

- regeneration may support C(t);

- but none of these alone replaces C(t) > P(t).

Final distinction:

S(t) is instantaneous synthesis of positive structural work.

W_S(T) is accumulated positive structural work.

Δ(t) is formal structural balance.

C(t) is general endogenous structural coherence.

Regeneration supports C(t), but is not identical to C(t).

Synchronization may support C(t), but is not identical to C(t).

Resonance may support C(t), but is not identical to C(t).

Real dynamic stability over time remains governed by:

C(t) > P(t)
