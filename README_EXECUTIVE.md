# EDS/EDC — Endogenous Dynamic Stability with Endogenous Dynamic Criticality

## Executive Summary

Real systems are not static structures.

They are dynamically retained operational processes sustained through continuous interaction with their environment.

This work introduces a unified operational framework describing:

- how systems preserve formal structural existence;
- how systems retain real dynamic stability over time;
- how systems approach and undergo critical transitions;
- how synchronization may support endogenous structural coherence without being identical to it;
- how structural regeneration supports C(t), but does not replace C(t);
- how dynamically stable regimes form, persist, degrade, or reorganize.

## Operational Parameter Definitions

S(t) — instantaneous intensity of synthesis of positive structural work at time t.

W_S(T) — accumulated positive structural work over an operational time interval T.

Δ(t) — instantaneous structural balance:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

P(t) — destabilizing structural pressure, operational load, fragmentation pressure, or operational destabilization at time t.

D(t) — irreversible structural losses and entropy-producing degradation at time t.

C(t) — parameter of general endogenous structural coherence determining the level of structural integrity and dynamic stability over time.

R(t) — operational synchronization order parameter representing measurable phase synchronization.

Structural regeneration is a continuous endogenous process of restoring and maintaining structural integrity and coherence over time.

Regeneration supports C(t), but is not identical to C(t).

Synchronization, resonance, phase alignment, and recursive continuity may support endogenous structural coherence.

They are not identical to C(t).

Synchronization must not be equated with coherence.

## Core Principle

Stability is not static equilibrium.

Stability is the ability of a system to retain structural integrity and dynamic stability over time through sufficient endogenous structural coherence.

Formal structural existence is described by:

Δ(t) = S(t) − P(t) − D(t)

where:

S(t) — instantaneous intensity of synthesis of positive structural work;

P(t) — destabilizing structural pressure;

D(t) — irreversible structural losses.

Positive Δ(t) supports formal structural existence.

But positive Δ(t) does not by itself prove real dynamic stability over time.

Real dynamic stability over time is described by:

C(t) > P(t)

where:

C(t) — parameter of general endogenous structural coherence determining structural integrity and dynamic stability over time;

P(t) — destabilizing structural pressure.

Operational interpretation:

general endogenous structural coherence must exceed destabilizing structural pressure for real dynamic stability over time.

## Mechanism

Any retained operational process requires a coherent internal structure of interaction.

Within EDS/EDC, the core mechanism is:

S(t) defines the instantaneous intensity of synthesis of positive structural work.

W_S(T) describes accumulated positive structural work over time:

W_S(T) = ∫[t₀ → t₁] S(t) dt

Accumulated positive structural work may support structural integrity, self-organization, and endogenous structural coherence.

Structural regeneration is a continuous endogenous process of restoring and maintaining structural integrity and coherence.

Regeneration supports C(t), but it is not C(t).

If endogenous processes are decoherent in phase, amplitude, accumulation rate, connection quality, or functional contribution, the structure loses the capacity to regenerate structural integrity faster than dissipation destroys it.

Root chain:

decoherence of endogenous processes → reduction of regenerative capacity → dissipation exceeds regeneration → decrease of C(t) → disruption of dynamic stability over time.

## EDC Transition Layer

EDC describes the regime near the stability boundary:

C(t) ≈ P(t)

At this boundary, the system may enter a critical regime where endogenous drift, nonlinear saturation, delayed transition, and scaling behavior become dominant.

The reduced EDC dynamics may be represented as:

dC/dt = rC − C³

where:

C(t) — endogenous structural coherence variable in the reduced critical regime;

r(t) — endogenous operational control parameter;

−C³ — nonlinear saturation term.

This equation does not redefine C(t) as regeneration.

C(t) remains the endogenous coherence variable.

Near criticality:

r(t) ≈ v_eff t

dC/dt = v_eff t C − C³

## Key Result

External coherent forcing does not mechanically impose structural stability.

Instead, it may:

- increase synchronization accessibility;
- reduce effective phase dispersion;
- support endogenous structural coherence;
- help the system enter a regime where retained coherence becomes more accessible.

But the decisive condition remains:

C(t) > P(t)

Synchronization can support real dynamic stability only if it contributes to the preservation of endogenous structural coherence above destabilizing pressure.

## Thesis

Retained structural continuity is not mechanically imposed.

It emerges through endogenous structural coherence within open nonlinear dynamical systems.

A system may preserve formal structural existence through positive structural balance:

Δ(t) > 0

but it remains dynamically stable over time only while:

C(t) > P(t)

Systems evolve, degrade, reorganize, or collapse according to the interaction between:

- synthesis of positive structural work;
- accumulated positive structural work;
- destabilizing pressure;
- irreversible losses;
- endogenous structural coherence;
- regenerative capacity;
- and critical regime drift.
